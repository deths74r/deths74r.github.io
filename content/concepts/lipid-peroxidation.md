**Lipid peroxidation** is the oxidative degradation of lipids — specifically, the
free-radical chain reaction that attacks the carbon–carbon double bonds of polyunsaturated
fatty acids (PUFAs). Because each double bond makes a neighbouring C–H bond easier to break,
the more unsaturated a fatty acid is, the more susceptible it is to this process.

The reaction proceeds in three classic stages.

## Initiation

A reactive species — for example a hydroxyl radical ($\text{HO}^{\bullet}$) — abstracts a
bis-allylic hydrogen from a polyunsaturated fatty acid ($\text{LH}$), producing a
carbon-centred lipid radical:

$$\text{LH} + \text{HO}^{\bullet} \rightarrow \text{L}^{\bullet} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

## Propagation

The lipid radical reacts rapidly with molecular oxygen to form a lipid peroxyl radical,
which in turn abstracts a hydrogen from another fatty acid — regenerating a lipid radical and
propagating the chain:

$$\text{L}^{\bullet} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{LOO}^{\bullet}$$

$$\text{LOO}^{\bullet} + \text{LH} \rightarrow \text{LOOH} + \text{L}^{\bullet}$$

A single initiating event can therefore oxidize many fatty acid molecules before it is
stopped.

## Termination

The chain ends when two radicals combine, or when an antioxidant such as α-tocopherol
(vitamin E) donates a hydrogen to quench a peroxyl radical, forming a comparatively stable
radical that does not propagate the chain.

## Why it matters

Lipid peroxidation produces reactive breakdown products — including aldehydes such as
malondialdehyde and 4-hydroxynonenal — that can modify proteins and DNA. It is implicated in
membrane damage, the oxidation of LDL particles, and a range of pathological processes. It is
also, importantly, a normal and tightly regulated feature of healthy biology, not merely a
form of damage.
