The story of seed oils in the modern diet is, before it is anything else, a story about
industrial capacity. A fat that was difficult to extract in 1900 was, by mid-century,
abundant and cheap. What changed was not human physiology but chemistry, agriculture, and
manufacturing — and the dietary advice that followed those changes deserves to be read in
that context.

## From byproduct to staple

The industrialization of oilseed processing turned what had been minor agricultural
byproducts into a dominant source of dietary fat.[^1] Understanding the debate requires
first understanding this shift in supply — the economics preceded the epidemiology.

## The mechanistic questions

Much of the contemporary argument turns on a small number of mechanistic claims about
polyunsaturated fats — chiefly linoleic acid — and their susceptibility to oxidation. These
are questions with real biochemistry behind them, and they are worth separating carefully
from the epidemiological claims they are often bundled with. See the concept note on
[lipid peroxidation]({{< relref "concepts/lipid-peroxidation" >}}) for the underlying
chemistry.

> The mechanism and the epidemiology are different arguments, evaluated with different kinds
> of evidence. Conflating them is the most common error on both sides.

## What a careful reading requires

A useful history separates three threads that are easy to tangle:

1. **Supply** — what changed in production and availability.
2. **Recommendation** — what dietary guidance was issued, when, and on what basis.
3. **Mechanism** — what the biochemistry does and does not establish.

Each thread has its own evidence and its own uncertainties. The paid newsletter companion to
this essay goes deeper into the archival record behind the recommendations.[^2]
