For more than twenty years I have worked as a histotechnologist—preparing, staining, and
reading tissue at the microscopic level, where diagnosis lives in the details. Context is
everything: in tissue, in data, in life.

I came up through the military. I began as a histopathology technician in the U.S. Air Force
and graduated with honors from the Tri-Service School of Histotechnology at the Armed Forces
Institute of Pathology (AFIP), Walter Reed. AFIP's standards became mine, and I have carried
them through every laboratory since.

The path has been an unusually varied one. I have supervised anatomic pathology and trained
new technicians across the Veterans Health Administration. I served as the sole
histotechnologist inside a Biosafety Level 3 laboratory at the USDA's Plum Island Animal
Disease Center, developing and optimizing immunohistochemistry protocols for foot-and-mouth
disease virus—seeking and validating new antibodies, designing experiments, and cutting well
over a hundred cryosections a day. More recently I worked in Mohs histology, preparing frozen
sections for real-time intraoperative margin evaluation and refining MART-1 immunohistochemistry
for melanoma cases.

Somewhere along the way the bench work became inseparable from the questions behind it. During
the early COVID-19 pandemic I worked with an interdisciplinary team of scientists, biophysicists,
and physicians on prophylactic strategies before vaccines existed, developing a theoretical
framework connecting molecular hypoxia to viral targets. It clarified where I am most useful:
where laboratory craft meets mechanism, and where you have to reason carefully from evidence
about systems too complex to simply intuit.

This site is where I write that reasoning down. Twenty years at the microscope taught me to look
closer, question assumptions, and find the signal in the noise. The essays here—on adaptive
systems, essential fatty acid biochemistry, thyroid physiology, and hormetic endocrinology—are an
extension of the same discipline: careful observation, honest uncertainty, and a refusal to
mistake a compelling story for a mechanism.
